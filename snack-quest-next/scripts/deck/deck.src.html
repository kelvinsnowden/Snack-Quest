The opportunity

Kenyans are discovering global snack culture online, and can’t easily buy it offline.

TikTok, Instagram and YouTube put Japanese, Korean, American, British and European snacks in front of Kenyan consumers every day. The demand is created online. The supply has not caught up.

Buying it today is hard

  • Fragmented. A handful of specialty sellers, scattered across platforms.
  • Inconsistent. Stock appears and disappears without warning.
  • Hard to discover. No single place to browse what exists.
  • Poorly suited to impulse. Social discovery is instant; buying is not.
  • Expensive and opaque. Prices vary widely between informal sellers.

What consumers actually want

Novelty Discovery Gifting Social experiences The thing they saw online International brands Something the supermarket doesn’t have

The curiosity already exists and is growing. Nobody has organised it into a brand a customer can return to.

Four orders is a small number and we are not going to dress it up. It is here because the shape matters more than the size, and the shape cuts both ways. The average order came in at KSh 4,875 against the KSh 3,500 the store model assumes, which is better than plan. Margin went the other way: 25.9% gross across the four, against a 45% target, and one order sold below cost. Order-level margin ranged from −21% to +39%. No repeat rate yet; four days does not produce one.

That spread is the honest headline. We do not yet know our own cost structure, and that is the single clearest reason this deck asks for KSh 500,000 before it asks for KSh 10M.

Operating spine Partners, not headcount

Product in, orders out, without building either.

The two things that usually sink a small retail operation are getting stock and getting it to the customer. Both are handled by named partners rather than by us hiring into them.

Supply: direct Korean sourcing

A supplier relationship that will source any snack from Korea and ship it to Kenya on a two-to-three month lead time.

It changes what the catalogue can be. Instead of selling whatever is already importable into Kenya, we can go after what customers are actually asking for on TikTok, including the products driving a trend before anyone else here stocks them.

Delivery: Tushop

A third-party logistics partner covering Nairobi and roughly 30km around it, on three service levels:

  • Express: collected and delivered within 90 minutes.
  • Same day: logged by 1pm, delivered by 6pm.
  • Next day: logged by midnight, delivered by 4pm.

What the Tushop terms do to the unit economics

Delivery becomes a formula, not a guess

KSh 35 per km of optimised route, plus 3% of declared order value for goods insurance.

That is a number we can put straight into the model rather than estimate. It also means the cost of serving a customer is known before we accept the order.

Batching rewards density

Three same-day orders sharing one optimised 20km route cost the same KSh 700 as a single drop on that route, not KSh 700 each.

Delivery cost per order therefore falls as order density rises. A store that concentrates demand in one part of Nairobi makes every delivery cheaper.

Insured in transit

100% of declared order value while the goods are in Tushop’s custody.

Live customer tracking

The customer gets a tracking link and the rider’s details, then a delivery confirmation and a review prompt.

No fleet, no capex

Monthly invoicing. No riders to hire, insure or manage as volume grows.

Today the company is two people. Tushop is the delivery team we would otherwise have to raise money to hire.

The founder full-time, plus one employee packing orders. Tushop’s terms and service levels are as set out above and the teams are aligning for kickoff; the Korean sourcing line is an established relationship rather than an exclusive contract. Both are stated as they stand.

What’s holding us back

Two constraints, and one location solves both.

These are not demand problems. They are infrastructure problems, and that is precisely what makes them fixable with this raise.

Payment friction

We are losing sales from customers who want to pay on delivery, or who simply aren’t comfortable paying online before they trust the brand.

A physical location creates a trusted place to buy: cash, card or M-Pesa, in person, with the product in hand.

Creator capacity

Creators want to work with us in exchange for product. We don’t yet have the physical space or operational capacity to host, shoot and fulfil at that volume.

The store becomes the content and activation hub: visits, shoots, launches and UGC production in one place.

The opportunity already exists. The constraint is our infrastructure and our ability to activate it at scale.

The model

Physical and digital, feeding each other.

The store does not compete with the website. It fills it.

1Discover: a creator’s video, or the store itself.
2Visit & buy: in person, no online payment hesitation.
3Experience: the snacks, the novelty, the surprise.
4Share: customers post it, and so do the creators.
5Reorder online: a curated box, delivered.
6Gift & repeat: boxes bought for others, and for new drops.
7Lifetime value grows, and the loop starts again with their audience.
Physical retail acquires and activates. Snack boxes retain and increase AOV.

AOV means average order value: what a customer spends per order. Boxes lift it because a box is many snacks bought in one decision.

Retention engine

The store acquires. The box is why they come back.

A single snack is a transaction. A curated box is a relationship: bigger basket, built-in reason to return, and a format that rotates forever.

Why boxes matter commercially

  • Bigger basket than buying snacks one at a time.
  • Repeat purchase built into the format.
  • Gifting: a box is a present, a packet isn’t.
  • Themed drops and seasonal campaigns create urgency.
  • Easy creator seeding: one box is one piece of content.

Future upside

  • Subscriptions: recurring boxes. Not live today, but a clear next step.
  • Frequent product rotation keeps the catalogue fresh without long-term SKU risk.
  • Corporate & occasion gifting at higher order values.

It funds paying on delivery

Customers arrive in two groups: those happy to pay online, and those who will not until they trust us. Serving the second group means shipping before being paid, and that takes working capital we do not currently have.

The float is what lets us offer it at all, and the number that decides the store case cannot be collected without the cash to collect it.

It borrows trust we have not built

The constraint on this business is trust, not interest. 177,800 people watched; four bought. A store closes that gap by being a real place you can walk into.

A creator closes it by lending credibility they have already earned. Stage one tests borrowed trust; stage two builds trust we own. Same hypothesis, and the cheap version is worth running first.

If those four clear, the store case stops being an argument and becomes arithmetic. If they do not, nobody has spent KSh 10M finding out.

We will also measure where people drop off: how many arrive from a creator video, reach checkout, and abandon at online payment. That figure is the evidence for a physical location, and it is the reason this test is designed to inform the store decision rather than replace it.
